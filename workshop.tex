\section{What the recogniser does}

A face photo is a grid of tiny squares called pixels, each with a brightness from
black to white. For every pixel, the recogniser looks at the eight pixels around it
and asks one simple question about each of them: is this neighbour brighter or darker
than me? A brighter neighbour becomes a 1, a darker one a 0. The eight answers form a
short code, like \texttt{10110010}, which is a little signature of that spot: an edge,
a line, or a flat area. The face is then divided into a grid of small windows, and in
each window the recogniser counts how often every signature appears. All those counts
together form a fingerprint of the face. To identify a person, the new fingerprint is
compared with the stored ones and the closest match wins. On ordinary photos this
works very well, recognising about 97.5\% of the faces correctly.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{The two problems}

Two everyday changes confuse this method. If one side of the face is much brighter
than the other, the ``brighter or darker than me?'' answer flips on the darker side, so
the signatures change and the accuracy falls to about 70\%. If the face is turned, the
little circle of eight neighbours turns with it, the digits of each signature shift
around, and at a 20-degree turn the accuracy drops to about 63\%. The rest of this note
is about the two fixes.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Fix 1: evening out the lighting}

The key idea is to remove the lighting \emph{before} the signatures are built, so the
signatures never see the bright-to-dark glow in the first place. The lighting across a
face is smooth and slowly changing, while the face itself has fine detail. So the
recogniser makes a \emph{blurry} copy of the photo, which keeps only the smooth glow
and loses the fine detail, and then divides the original photo by that blurry copy.
The smooth glow is the same in both, so it cancels out, while the fine face detail
survives the division. What is left is the face as if it had been lit perfectly evenly,
and only then are the signatures and the fingerprint computed.

This is the same trick a phone camera uses for automatic brightness correction, except
here it is applied to every part of the face separately, so a bright left side and a
dark right side both come out evenly lit. The only setting is how blurry the copy
should be (a blur width of about eight pixels), which is small enough to leave the eyes,
nose and mouth sharp but large enough to catch the lighting. Because this step only
removes smooth brightness, it does nothing to a normal, evenly lit photo, so the
accuracy on easy photos is not affected. This fix brings the strongly lit case back up
to about 97.5\%.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Fix 2: turning the faces while learning them}

Here the idea is to prepare for rotation when the faces are first stored. The
recogniser does not only keep the straight-on photo of each known person; it also keeps
several copies turned a little, one every five degrees from thirty degrees left to
thirty degrees right. Each copy is just another fingerprint in the database for the
same person. When a turned photo then arrives, it does not have to match a straight-on
template: it can match the turned copy that lines up with it, and so it is still
recognised as the right person.

This works like keeping a few passport photos of a friend, one facing straight, one
turned slightly left and one turned slightly right. Whichever way the friend is looking,
one of the photos still looks like them. Turning the stored faces is cheap and is done
once, ahead of time, and it does not change the straight-on photos at all, so normal
recognition is untouched. This fix brings the 20-degree case back up to about 98\%.

In short, two simple ideas fix the two problems. The lighting fix divides out the
smooth light so it does not fool the ``brighter or darker'' test, and the rotation fix
keeps a few turned copies of each known face so a turned photo still matches. Accuracy
on normal photos does not change, while the hard cases become almost as good as the
easy ones.
