\section{What the recogniser does}

A face photo is a grid of tiny squares called pixels, each with a brightness from
black to white. For every pixel, the recogniser looks at the eight pixels around it
and asks one simple question about each of them: is this neighbour brighter or darker
than me? A brighter neighbour becomes a 1, a darker one a 0. The eight answers form a
short code, like \texttt{10110010}, which is a little signature of that spot: an edge,
a line, or a flat area. The face is then divided into a grid of small windows, and in
each window the recogniser counts how often every signature appears. All those counts
together form a fingerprint of the face. To identify a person, the new fingerprint is
compared with the stored ones and the closest match wins. On ordinary photos this
works very well, recognising about 97.5\% of the faces correctly.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Why lighting changes break it}

If one side of the face is much brighter than the other, the ``brighter or darker than
me?'' answer flips on the darker side, so the signatures change. It is like reading a
page with a lamp shining on one side: the words are the same, but they look different.
The fingerprint changes even though the person has not, and the accuracy falls to about
70\%. To fix this, we even out the lighting before building the signatures. The
recogniser makes a blurry copy of the photo, which captures the smooth bright-to-dark
glow rather than the face details, and then divides the photo by that blurry copy. The
lighting cancels out and the face texture stays. It works like a phone camera's
automatic brightness correction, but applied to every part of the face so the whole
face looks evenly lit. This brings the accuracy back up to about 97.5\%.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Why rotation breaks it}

When the face is turned, the little circle of eight neighbours around each pixel turns
with it. The same signature now has its digits shifted around, like spinning a
combination lock, so the recogniser reads it as a different code even though it is the
same spot on the same face. At a 20-degree turn the accuracy drops to about 63\%. To
fix this, when the recogniser learns a face it stores not only the straight-on photo
but also copies turned a little, for example every five degrees from minus thirty to
plus thirty. When a turned photo arrives, it can then match a turned copy of the right
person. It is like keeping passport photos of a friend facing straight, slightly left
and slightly right, so you still recognise them whichever way they are looking. This
raises the accuracy to about 98\%.

  \end{tabular}

In short, two simple ideas fix the two problems. The lighting fix divides out the
smooth light so it does not fool the ``brighter or darker'' test, and the rotation fix
keeps a few turned copies of each known face so a turned photo still matches. Accuracy
on normal photos does not change, while the hard cases become almost as good as the
easy ones.
